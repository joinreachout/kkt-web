There is a lot of understandable FOMO around AI. For the last two years, attention was mostly on technology — GenAI, copilots, RAG, reasoning models, and then Agents. Now I see the conversation in the Business AI world shifting towards "AI-native" companies, operating models and organisational transformation.

Part of that shift is right. Across the markets where we work — from the EU and the Caucasus to Southeast Asia — capable AI engineering is becoming much easier to find. Building reliable production systems is still hard, but technology itself is increasingly only one part of the problem.

McKinsey, BCG and PwC are now focusing much more on processes, people and operating models. I particularly like EY's distinction between modernization, innovation and transformation: different types of change rather than stages every company must eventually go through.

But there is also an obvious bias worth acknowledging: AI transformation is a much larger engagement to sell than five focused process improvements. I say this as someone who sells both.

For many established companies, a sensible AI strategy may therefore look more like this: improve ten processes, redesign two, transform one. Not transform everything.

## Two strategy sessions, two different answers

Earlier this year, we ran several AI strategy sessions with a global testing, inspection and certification company operating in more than 70 countries. There was a real opportunity to use decades of testing data and technical expertise to move further up the value chain — from issuing results and certificates towards predictive insights, expert AI services and new intelligence products. In simple terms, from "we tell you what happened" towards "we help you decide what to do next."

But as we went deeper, a lot of immediate value turned out to be much closer to the existing business: digitising workflows, reducing manual work and making specialist knowledge easier to access. The company could get substantial value from AI without reinventing itself.

A few months later, we had an AI strategy session with a large home-improvement retailer in Asia, and the picture was almost the opposite. The company already had meaningful data, ML and AI capabilities, while its strategy was moving beyond simply selling products towards building a broader relationship around the customer's home — products, digital channels, delivery, installation and services.

Here AI gets much closer to how the company wants to compete. Understanding one customer across the store, app, delivery, installation and ongoing services is not simply a better marketing workflow; AI starts changing how those parts of the business need to work together. That is much closer to genuine transformation.

## The distinction I keep coming back to

![Choose the right level of change: improve ten, redesign two, transform one. Digitise workflows where AI improves the work; redesign processes where AI changes the work; transform the business where AI changes how the company wins.](/blog/ai-levels-of-change-v1.png)

**Digitise** where AI improves the work.

**Redesign** where AI changes the work.

**Transform** where AI changes how the company wins.

Getting that level wrong has a practical cost. A company can spend months of senior-management attention changing roles, reporting lines and incentives around an "AI transformation" when much of the same value could have been captured through a handful of focused changes. That is not more ambitious. It is more expensive.

For me, good AI strategy is about changing the right things.
